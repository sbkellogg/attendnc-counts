What We Know About Attendance

What research tells us about why students miss school, why it matters, and what schools can do about it.

The evidence in brief

Attendance matters for learning, but the strongest response is rarely a single attendance intervention. The research favors coordinated, data-informed systems that combine relationships with practical barrier removal.

Five conclusions

What the evidence says

Across the literature review and recent North Carolina studies, five findings are especially useful for school and district leaders.

1

Missing school still matters.

Recent North Carolina analyses find measurable academic costs from additional days absent, even after accounting for prior achievement and other factors.

2

The 10% threshold hides important differences.

Some students experience a temporary attendance problem. Others are absent deeply and persistently across multiple years and may need far more intensive support.

3

Absence is multicausal.

Attendance patterns can reflect school climate, anxiety, family routines, transportation, housing, economic stress, disengagement, health, and other interacting conditions.

4

Relationships are essential, but not sufficient.

Trusted relationships help schools learn what is happening. They are most useful when that information leads to concrete support for the barrier behind the absence.

5

Implementation matters as much as the strategy.

Low-lift messages can help, but sustained improvement depends on clear roles, timely data, follow-through, adequate capacity, and fit with local context.

A closer look

Why students miss school

Absence is often the visible result of several overlapping conditions. The research points to factors at the student, school, family, and structural levels.

Student experiencesAnxiety and other mental-health concerns, low self-concept, academic stress, peer difficulties, disengagement, and school refusal can all contribute to avoidance.
School experienceStudents are somewhat less likely to attend when they feel unsafe, disconnected, or unsupported. Weak adult relationships and exclusionary discipline can compound the problem.
Family and logistical barriersTransportation, unstable housing, limited access to health care, family routines, and economic instability can make regular attendance difficult even when students and caregivers value school.
Broader conditionsPoverty, school resources, transportation policy, and other community or policy conditions can shape who is able to attend consistently and which students face persistent barriers.

A closer look

What repeated absence can affect

The consequences extend beyond the lessons a student misses on a given day. They accumulate across learning, engagement, classrooms, and longer-term outcomes.

Learning and attainmentMore absence is associated with lower test scores, grades, and proficiency, as well as greater risks of course failure, grade retention, and dropping out.
Engagement and connectionRepeated absence can weaken academic routines and students’ connections to peers, teachers, and school, making re-engagement increasingly difficult.
Classrooms and schoolsHigh levels of absence disrupt instructional continuity and planning, increase intervention demands on staff, and can affect classmates as teachers revisit missed material or peers disengage.
Longer-term opportunityThe evidence links persistent absence to lower educational attainment and risks to postsecondary participation, career readiness, and later economic opportunity.

A useful shift in perspective: The research does not point to a universal “best attendance program.” It points toward a repeatable process: identify risk early, understand the cause, match the response, monitor whether it works, and intensify support when needed.

From evidence to action

What shows promise

The NCDPI review found evidence for a range of approaches, but generally with modest effects and substantial variation in implementation. The strongest practical lesson is to combine strategies within a coordinated system rather than rely on one in isolation.

01

Notice early and review often

Use timely attendance data, early warning indicators, and a multidisciplinary attendance or student-support team to identify emerging patterns before they become entrenched.

Promising evidence
02

Ask what is behind the absence

Pair quantitative data with direct student and family inquiry. The same number of absences can reflect very different needs, so the response should begin with understanding rather than assumption.

Core implementation principle
03

Match support to the barrier

Promising responses include targeted caregiver messaging, home visits, mentoring and trusted-adult supports, transportation assistance, mental health supports, and connections to community resources.

Context dependent
04

Strengthen belonging and reasons to attend

School connectedness, supportive adult and peer relationships, positive climate, relevant learning, and meaningful activities can make attendance more personally valuable to students.

Relational + instructional
05

Use a multi-tiered response

Universal supports can establish an attendance culture, targeted supports can respond to emerging risk, and intensive individualized supports can address deep or persistent absence.

Well-aligned with MTSS
06

Track whether the response works

Attendance improvement requires a feedback loop. Schools need to know which supports were delivered, whether attendance changed, and when the plan should be adjusted or intensified.

Implementation essential

What the research cautions against

  • Legal or punitive responses as the first strategy. They often fail to address the underlying reason a student is absent.
  • Generic reminders as a complete intervention. Letters, calls, and text messages can help, but their effects are usually modest when used alone.
  • Relationship-building without barrier removal. Repeated outreach can feel intrusive when families identify transportation, health, safety, or economic conditions that remain unresolved.
  • Fragmented or shallow implementation. Promising practices can fail when schools lack time, staffing, clear roles, coordination, or a way to monitor progress.
  • One-size-fits-all solutions. Strategies that work in one setting can produce weak or unintended results when copied without attention to context.

Evidence from North Carolina

Recent Evidence from North Carolina

Three recent studies using North Carolina administrative data sharpen the picture. Together they show that absenteeism is both more widespread and more persistent than before the pandemic, and that missing school continues to carry measurable academic costs.

Longitudinal descriptive study

Student-Level Attendance Patterns Across Three Post-Pandemic Years

Post-pandemic absence is widespread, but a smaller group of students is experiencing especially deep and persistent absence.

Among students followed across three years, 38% were chronically absent at least once post-pandemic compared with 17% before the pandemic. The share chronically absent in all three years rose from 2.4% to 9.6%.

Persistent absence was more common among Black and Hispanic students and students in high-poverty schools.

Swiderski, Fuller & BastianEducational Evaluation and Policy Analysis, 2026

North Carolina achievement study

The Relationship Between Student Attendance and Achievement, Pre- and Post-COVID

Each additional day absent in 2023–24 was associated with about a 0.0057 SD decline in math achievement.

Students were still missing about three more days than comparable pre-pandemic students. The authors estimate that returning attendance to pre-pandemic norms could have closed roughly 13% to 19% of the remaining statewide math achievement gap in 2023–24.

The study also found evidence that classmates’ absences can affect peers’ achievement.

Swiderski, Fuller & BastianAERA Open, 2025
View the study →
Causal analysis, includes NC

The Causal Effect of Student Absences Post Pandemic

Absences remained harmful after the pandemic, even though the estimated effect was somewhat smaller in 2022–23 than before COVID.

Across North Carolina, Maryland, and a large urban district, the effects were approximately linear. Ten absences reduced math scores by roughly 0.04 to 0.07 standard deviations in elementary and middle school.

The central implication is simple: the academic cost of absence did not disappear after COVID.

Extending the research base

What Bright Spots Add

Research can identify strategies associated with improved attendance, but it often tells us less about how those strategies are combined, adapted, and sustained in real school settings. AttendNC Bright Spots extends that evidence by learning directly from schools that are making unusual progress and examining the routines, relationships, decisions, and conditions that may be contributing to their improvement.

From “what works?” to “how does it work here?”

That distinction is central to AttendNC. The literature can identify promising strategies, but schools still have to fit those strategies to students, families, staffing, routines, and local barriers.

Bright Spots makes implementation visible.

Research: Early identificationBright Spots: daily monitoring, weekly team review, clear thresholds, and rapid follow-up.
Research: RelationshipsBright Spots: adults who know students, families, histories, and when something has changed.
Research: Barrier reductionBright Spots: practical responses to transportation, routines, housing, food, anxiety, and other needs.
Research: ConnectednessBright Spots: belonging, recognition, meaningful programs, relevant learning, and reasons to be at school.
Research: MTSS and coordinationBright Spots: differentiated supports, named owners, shared information, and escalation when needed.
Research: Implementation fidelityBright Spots: consistent routines, leadership attention, documentation, and persistent follow-through.

About the evidence base

How the NCDPI review was conducted

The literature review was designed to surface recent, educator-actionable evidence on chronic absenteeism in K–12 settings and to distinguish school-level practices from broader structural conditions.

Systematic review process

  • PRISMA-aligned search and screening process
  • Academic databases including ProQuest, ERIC/EBSCO, Web of Science, PsycINFO, and Academic Search Complete
  • Peer-reviewed studies published from 2020 through 2025
  • 221 records initially identified; 48 studies/reports included after screening
  • Focus on general-education K–12 settings

Important scope note

North Carolina’s operational definition of chronic absence includes excused, unexcused, and disciplinary absences. The NCDPI literature review intentionally used a narrower evidence-search strategy focused primarily on behaviorally driven, unexcused patterns such as school refusal and truancy, and excluded much of the health-related absence literature.

The North Carolina studies summarized above use broader administrative absence measures and therefore complement the review.
