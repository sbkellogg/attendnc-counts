School Spotlight

A snapshot of Bearfield Primary School’s attendance progress

Bearfield Primary has built an attendance system designed to identify problems early, connect students with caring adults, and remove barriers before absences become entrenched. Weekly student-level data review, first-absence family outreach, assigned adult check-ins, root-cause investigation, practical supports, and persistent follow-through work together as a coordinated routine.

The approach is deliberately preventive. Staff do not wait for a student to become chronically absent before acting. Weekly data help the team identify students whose attendance is beginning to worsen, while daily check-ins give students a specific adult who notices when they are present and follows up when they are not.

The result is a data-informed relationship and problem-solving system: data identify who needs attention, relationships reveal what is happening, and the team organizes a response around what each student and family need.

Behind the Absence: A Family Story

When housing instability became the attendance barrier

Housing instabilitySometimes attendance is the visible symptom of a much larger family crisis.

A family experiencing housing instability was moving among temporary locations. The disruption affected transportation, morning routines, punctuality, hygiene, and the caregiver’s ability to keep school attendance consistent. Rather than treating the pattern as simple noncompliance, Bearfield staff worked to stabilize the circumstances around the children, connecting the family with school, district, faith-based, and community resources for housing, clothing, food, and transportation.

What this illustrates: The attendance record identified a problem, but the response depended on understanding the family’s circumstances and addressing the conditions that were making regular attendance difficult.

Practices behind the progress

Six connected strategies

Bearfield’s attendance approach is built around a set of mutually reinforcing practices. None stands alone. Together, they move the school from noticing a pattern to understanding it, responding, and checking whether the response worked.

Weekly Early-Warning Review

The team reviews student-level data each week and tracks movement across risk levels. Particular attention goes to students trending toward chronic absence while there is still time to change the trajectory.

What another school could try: Create simple weekly risk levels and focus first on students whose attendance is worsening but not yet deeply entrenched.

First-Absence Outreach and Documentation

Teachers are expected to contact families after the first absence, often by phone or ClassDojo. Contacts and key information are documented so the team can see what has already been learned and what still needs follow-up.

What another school could try: Make the first contact supportive: “We noticed your child was not here. Is everything okay, and is there anything we can do to help?”

Assigned Adult Check-Ins

Students at elevated risk are assigned to attendance-team members who greet them, tell them they are glad to see them, and notice when they are missing. The interactions are brief, predictable, and personal.

What another school could try: Give each targeted student one named adult who provides a quick positive check-in whenever the student is present.

Root-Cause Investigation

The team treats an attendance count as a signal, not a diagnosis. Staff ask about transportation, housing, caregiver work schedules, family conflict, health, morning routines, and other circumstances before choosing a response.

What another school could try: Add one standing question to every attendance review: “What do we know about why this student is missing?”

Barrier-Specific Support

The response changes with the problem. Staff may work with district transportation, relatives, neighboring families, community organizations, churches, McKinney-Vento supports, food programs, or other resources to remove the actual barrier.

What another school could try: Organize local attendance supports by barrier rather than by number of absences alone.

Persistent Follow-Through and Escalation

Support is not a single phone call. The team revisits students each week, changes course when a strategy is not working, and escalates to family meetings, home visits, formal plans, or referral only when earlier efforts do not resolve the concern.

What another school could try: End each student discussion with a named owner, a next action, and a date to check whether attendance changed.

How the system works

From weekly data to daily connection

Bearfield’s workflow links early warning, relationships, practical problem-solving, and reassessment. The formal team meeting creates the structure, while much of the actual intervention happens throughout the week.

1

Identify

Weekly data surface students who are absent repeatedly or moving toward chronic absence.

2

Assign

The team prioritizes students and assigns a specific adult to notice and connect with them.

3

Connect

Teachers and assigned adults greet students when present and contact families quickly when absent.

4

Understand

Staff gather context from the student, family, teachers, and team to identify the root cause.

5

Respond

The school matches support and the person making contact to the specific barrier.

6

Reassess

The team reviews the student again, reduces support when attendance improves, or intensifies it when needed.

First absenceTeacher outreach communicates that the student was noticed and asks what happened.
3-6 absencesAttendance letters and more direct outreach reinforce expectations and identify barriers.
~10 absencesFamily meeting, documented concern, and a more specific plan or support.
Persistent concernHome visits, social-worker involvement, and formal escalation when supportive efforts have not resolved the problem.

Formal notification thresholds may vary depending on the circumstances and stage of intervention, but Bearfield’s supportive outreach begins well before a student becomes chronically absent.

Conditions for success

At Bearfield, different adults bring different knowledge to the same students

Principal and assistant principal

Protect attendance-team time, review data, participate directly in student check-ins and family contact, coordinate transportation and district support, and reinforce attendance as an instructional priority.

School counselor and social worker

Investigate family circumstances, build trust, connect caregivers with resources, support home visits, and help the team understand barriers involving housing, family conflict, transportation, or other needs.

School nurse and data-focused team members

Translate weekly attendance reports into usable risk categories, track movement over time, test possible explanations, and help the team see whether a student’s trajectory is improving.

Data manager

Generates attendance information and letters, helps identify students crossing formal thresholds, and provides the records the team uses for recurring review.

Teachers and instructional staff

Enter attendance, notice emerging patterns, make first-absence contact, share context about students, document communication, and reinforce the message that students are wanted and needed at school.

Families, transportation, and community partners

Relatives, neighboring caregivers, district transportation, churches, food programs, housing supports, and other local organizations expand the school’s ability to respond to practical barriers.

What other schools can adapt

Start by connecting data to a specific adult response

Six practical starting points

  1. 1

    Put names behind the dataMove beyond a schoolwide rate and routinely identify which individual students are beginning to miss school.

  2. 2

    Reach out on the first absenceUse early contact to communicate concern and learn what happened before a pattern becomes established.

  3. 3

    Assign a caring adultGive targeted students a specific person who notices when they arrive, welcomes them, and follows up when they are missing.

  4. 4

    Ask why before respondingTreat transportation, housing, family routines, health, and other barriers as different problems requiring different supports.

  5. 5

    Match support and messengerUse the staff member, relative, district service, or community partner best positioned to help with the specific circumstance.

  6. 6

    Review and adjust weeklyUse recent attendance and staff observations to continue, reduce, intensify, or replace the current strategy.

What to consider

Many of Bearfield’s core practices are low-cost and adaptable, but they depend on organizational conditions that other schools may need to recreate differently.

  • Bearfield serves a large rural area where transportation and long bus rides are recurring attendance barriers.
  • The school protects approximately an hour each week for a multidisciplinary attendance team.
  • Long-serving staff bring substantial knowledge of local families, relatives, and community resources.
  • The check-in model requires manageable adult caseloads and consistent follow-through, even when staff have competing responsibilities.
  • Community, faith-based, transportation, housing, food, and family resources extend what the school can provide directly.
  • These practices should be understood as promising approaches associated with Bearfield’s improvement, not causal evidence that any single practice produced the change.

Evidence and interpretation

What this spotlight can and cannot establish

This is a promising-practice profile, not an impact evaluation. Bearfield Primary School was selected because its attendance improvement merited further inquiry. The practices and conditions highlighted here offer plausible explanations for that progress, but they do not establish that any one practice caused the decline. The most transferable lesson is the interaction among early identification, assigned adult relationships, rapid family outreach, root-cause investigation, barrier-specific support, and persistent follow-through.
